# Tony Kim: AI's Next Winners

## TLDR

BlackRock technology investor Tony Kim argues AI reversed technology's value stack: compute, memory, networking, power and physical infrastructure now capture more value than old asset-light software. He keeps roughly `90%` of his focus inside the next three-year AI “vortex,” while reserving a minority for asymmetric 2030 bets such as quantum, orbital data centers, SMRs, 800V power and robotics.

Core risk: many hard numbers are Tony's rough framing, not audited forecasts. Demand growth does not establish supplier margins or attractive stock valuations.

He discloses no holdings, weights, entry valuations, expected returns, risk limits or performance. Treat this as framework—not BlackRock portfolio disclosure.

## Compute-centric rebuild `[03:52]`

Tony says AI changed data centers from CPU-plus-storage clouds into capital-heavy “token factories”:

- Servers moved from roughly `$10K` to `$1M+` systems.
- Data-center scale moved from megawatts to gigawatts.
- He estimates around `$1T` AI capex this year and `$10T` over five years.
- His rough market-cap map: `$10T+` software/services/internet, `$22–23T` Mag 7 and `$30T+` chips/hardware outside Mag 7.
- AI workloads move data over centimeters/millimeters, creating bandwidth, heat and power bottlenecks.
- Architecture shifts include copper → optics, 800V power, solid-state transformers, advanced packaging and model-chip co-design.

His economic thesis: old cloud used cheap commodity compute to support high-margin SaaS. AI requires expensive compute sold as tokens, moving capital and some margin down stack. Underpinning assumption: intelligence capability compounds roughly one order of magnitude/year, better models create more use cases, and added use more than offsets efficiency gains.

Caveat: hardware market-cap comparison groups broad categories and does not prove incremental returns on today's capex. If capability, adoption or monetization lags—or efficiency dominates Jevons demand—the argument weakens.

## Memory thesis `[10:44]`

Tony expects memory intensity to rise as agents accumulate personal and enterprise context:

- Models need DRAM, HBM, SRAM and storage close to compute.
- Host says supply is concentrated among three major memory vendors; Tony agrees.
- New fabs take roughly `3–4 years`, creating duration mismatch with current shortages.
- Memory-chip-model co-design becomes important.

He calls this “RAMpocalypse.” Structural idea sound—context, KV cache and agent state increase bytes moved/stored—but scarcity can reverse after synchronized capacity additions. Track bit demand, HBM mix, contract pricing, utilization, yields and WFE/fab expansion.

## Capital-allocation framework `[16:03]`

- Roughly `90%` in ideas with visible three-year AI demand.
- Require relevance beyond forecast window because year-five perception determines exit multiple.
- Ask: **“Will this still be cool in five years?”**
- Small frontier allocation seeks nonlinear upside where timing/utility converge around 2030.
- Avoid businesses that look cheap because relevance is decaying.

This is duration-barbell investing: underwrite near-term cash generation while preserving exposure to convex technology outcomes.

## Chips were never commodities `[38:16]`

Tony argues semiconductors have unusually high margins because:

- categories consolidated into oligopolies/duopolies;
- capital and technical barriers rose;
- venture funding avoided hardware for years;
- surviving firms gained pricing power.

AI revives physical disciplines: chip/memory design, optics, materials, packaging, cooling, racks and power.

Caveat: industry concentration helps suppliers, but cycle, customer insourcing and capacity overshoot remain. High historic margin does not make every chip company a moat.

## Robotics `[43:30]`

Tony splits humanoid stack into:

- **brain:** LLM/language layer plus world/motor model;
- **body:** actuators, hands, manufacturing and supply chain.

He says China has roughly `130–140` robotics companies and may see `30–40` IPOs this year, versus few in US. He notes shallower Chinese private markets push companies public earlier, so IPO count is not maturity proof. His expected hybrid: Chinese/Asian low-cost bodies plus Western models.

Contrarian opportunity is social robots—not only factories:

- elderly companionship;
- education;
- loneliness;
- multilingual, empathetic assistants;
- life-story capture.

He argues social utility may not require perfect hands or full-size humanoid form.

Risks: private claims/IPO count need verification; Western-brain/Chinese-body integration faces export controls, data/security rules, tariffs, safety and weak willingness-to-pay. Companionship benefit remains product hypothesis.

## Enterprise “token flow” `[53:07]`

Tony's future enterprise stack:

1. Compute creates tokens.
2. Foundation models package intelligence.
3. Company data/context/ontology encodes proprietary knowledge.
4. Agents act through that context.
5. Apps either resell/repackage tokens or sell complete outcomes.

His test: **Is company inside token flow, and where can it retain margin?**

Potential winners own compute, model, proprietary context/data, distribution or end-to-end outcome. Thin wrappers risk margin compression. PE/venture may buy traditional companies, replace workflows with AI and sell cheaper outcomes—buying customer distribution rather than selling tools into resistant incumbents.

Tony illustrates outcome pricing as replacing a service costing `100` with AI-native delivery costing `20`; no company, implementation cost or audited savings support example. “Token flow” is usage map, not automatic moat: model deflation, caching, routing, open-source substitution and customer bargaining power can leave high volume with low margin. Tony says roll-up jury remains out.

## Frontier bets `[32:41]`

Tony sees many technologies converging near 2030:

- useful fault-tolerant quantum computing;
- orbital data centers;
- SMRs and possibly later fusion;
- 800V data-center power;
- solid-state transformers;
- AGI-class systems.

These are scenario targets, not forecasts. Shared 2030 storytelling may reflect financing narratives more than synchronized technical readiness.

Podcast supplies no quantum error-correction economics, orbital launch/cooling/radiation model, SMR project milestones or power-electronics deployment evidence. Tony himself says ten-year forecasting is nearly impossible.

## Theory of edge

> AI creates structural scarcity in compute, memory, networking, optics, power and physical engineering faster than supply and software-era investor frameworks adjust.

Potential payoff comes from underwriting bottlenecks and five-year relevance before consensus. Market may pay investor for accepting capex cyclicality, technological obsolescence and long lead times.

## Risks / kill tests

1. AI revenue fails to justify `$10T` infrastructure buildout.
2. Model efficiency reduces compute/memory demand faster than new use cases expand it.
3. Hyperscalers use purchasing power/custom silicon to capture supplier economics.
4. Memory/fab additions turn scarcity into oversupply.
5. Optical/800V/orbital/quantum schedules slip beyond investable horizon.
6. High hardware valuation already discounts bottleneck profits.
7. Token prices collapse faster than volumes rise.
8. Robotics demand stays industrial/niche rather than mass consumer.

## YK read-through

Best insight: AI thesis should be mapped through physical bottlenecks and token flow—not raw user count. Separate:

- demand growth;
- scarce layer;
- supplier bargaining power;
- capex/funding duration;
- margin after customer concentration;
- valuation already paid.

Tony gives useful map, not stock list. Strongest current baskets are memory/HBM, networking/optics, power/cooling and advanced packaging—only where capacity remains disciplined and supplier value capture survives hyperscaler pressure.

## Career note `[1:03:51]`

Tony says he lacked conventional mentors. A few people gave him latitude and believed in him; historical figures became intellectual mentors when he grew up socially isolated and spent time in libraries. His practical rule: give younger people a shot and room to act, not merely advice.

Career read-through: scarce physical-stack skills—memory architecture, semiconductor design, packaging, materials, power, thermal systems and model-hardware co-design—may have stronger scarcity than another generic software profile.
