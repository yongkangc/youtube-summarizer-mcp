# Josh Young: Finding Edge in Small-Cap Energy

## Source

- Video: [World's #1 Oil Hedge Fund Manager: How I Made 200% While Everyone Lost 60%](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F0g4mEFefN8)
- Show: Odds on Open
- Guest: Josh Young, Bison Interests
- Duration: 1:14:50
- Summary based on complete transcript.
- Performance, company, market-share and oil-price figures are speaker/interviewer claims. No audited return series or holdings accompany video.

## TLDR

Young says Bison returned roughly 200% since its 2015 launch while small-cap energy sector fell roughly 60%. That means $1 allegedly became $3 versus $0.40, or 7.5× relative ending wealth.

His edge is not forecasting oil. It is buying illiquid, neglected energy companies below liquidation/reserve/replacement value where debt repayment, capital-allocation improvement and operational inflection can compound equity value. He supplements filings with field intelligence, service-company checks and obscure public regulatory data.

Strategy remains concentrated and volatile. Young admits historical oversizing caused painful underperformance. Strong process lesson: chase edge, not recent returns—but performance claim needs audited verification.

## Theory of edge

Why market pays him:

- small-cap energy is institutionally abandoned, illiquid and ESG-constrained;
- fewer specialist funds remain to arbitrage mispricing;
- investors over-focus on commodity narratives and static free-cash-flow yields;
- obscure state/provincial filings contain public but poorly disseminated operating data;
- service companies and large producers reveal information transferable to nearby small caps;
- debt reduction can build equity value for years before market recognizes it;
- governance activism can force asset sales or better capital allocation.

This combines institutional constraint, information-processing edge and active control.

## Stock-selection process

Starting screen:

- large discount to likely liquidation value;
- discount to third-party reserve appraisal and replacement cost;
- survivable balance sheet;
- cash-generating or saleable assets;
- high prospective return on invested capital;
- management capable of rational capital allocation;
- misunderstood catalyst likely visible within 18–24 months.

He prefers businesses that can earn around 20% returns at $60 oil. Commodity path still affects mark-to-market results, but long-run thesis rests on debt repayment, buybacks, dividends, accretive acquisitions and NAV growth.

## Operational inflections

Best setups often look bad on trailing accounts because company is finishing infrastructure or development project. Once spending ends, capital shifts into high-return wells and free cash flow inflects.

Another pattern: leverage falls from roughly 4× debt/cash flow to below 1× while market capitalization barely moves. Recognition may arrive “slowly, then all at once,” followed by buybacks, dividends and multiple expansion.

## Macro and oil price

Young says short/medium-term oil forecasting is exceptionally difficult—even sophisticated derivatives market makers struggle. He estimates his own directional hit rate near 55%.

Therefore:

- no margin or portfolio leverage;
- no stock shorts;
- occasional tiny out-of-the-money puts/calls as asymmetric insurance;
- shift among oil, gas, midstream and services mainly from valuation and longer-run opportunity.

He thinks WTI could eventually reach roughly $250 because commodity cycles have historically peaked near inflation-adjusted prior highs. This is right-tail optionality, not core stock thesis.

## Position sizing

Young admits fundamental investors often pick better than they size.

Historical large positions magnified both strongest gains and worst drawdowns. He now uses:

- starter/speculative positions around 0.2%–2%;
- medium positions for clearer, steadier setups;
- large positions around 10%–12% at cost for extreme discounts and visible inflections;
- less willingness to exceed those sizes, though historical positions reached closer to 20%.

He historically averaged down but says most money came from adding on way up, when price confirmed thesis. Current goal: buy more after fundamental confirmation rather than mechanically treating lower price as lower risk.

## Why not short

Shorting introduces:

- unlimited/path-dependent loss;
- timing mismatch;
- emotional distraction disproportionate to size;
- risk sector beta rises while long book lags;
- weak fit with his demonstrated skill.

A past fraudulent-looking energy short rose around 10×. Even current 0.2% put basket consumes excessive attention. He prefers long-term compounding on long side.

## Activism

Young became chairman after Bison accumulated roughly 7% of a public E&P company. He helped replace board/management, negotiate an $80 million asset sale and position company for acquisition bidding.

Activism is reserved for rare cases where value gap is enormous. Most companies requiring activism are simply bad investments. Current approach keeps fund position near ordinary large size, around 10%, and raises separate co-investment capital rather than overloading core fund.

## Information edge example

A Canadian provincial report showed test wells with only one or two laterals versus expected eight. Headline production looked mediocre, but per-lateral output implied potentially strong full-development economics.

Market and banks allegedly failed simple normalization. Young bought heavily; stock later rose roughly 300% over 18 months. This is anecdotal and oil also rose, but illustrates method:

1. Find public data others ignore.
2. Normalize technical units correctly.
3. Compare against economic threshold.
4. Size when upside remains robust even after conservative haircut.

## Capital and positioning

Young argues principal-owned family offices understand volatile concentrated bets better than career allocators. Professional allocators avoid energy due to poor historical returns, ESG concerns and career risk—helping preserve mispricing.

He cites energy at about 4% of S&P 500 versus roughly 10% average over prior two decades and historical peaks near 27%. Tech is around 40%. Mean reversion toward 10% energy would provide powerful tailwind, but sector-weight history alone does not establish fair future allocation.

## AI

Current models help with:

- diligence acceleration;
- tracking changes;
- analyst productivity;
- document review.

They do not yet replace judgment across upstream, midstream, services, geology, operations and capital allocation. Young reports hallucination/consistency issues and says oil companies publicly promoting AI have not shown measurable operating benefit in his experience. These are observations, not systematic evidence.

## Risks and evidence gaps

- Performance is unaudited in video; benchmark identity and fee treatment unclear.
- Concentrated 10%+ positions create idiosyncratic drawdowns.
- Liquidation and reserve values depend heavily on commodity deck, decline curves, costs and buyer liquidity.
- Energy correlations converge during crashes.
- Activism consumes attention and introduces legal/governance risk.
- Illiquidity can create both edge and inability to exit.
- $250 oil thesis may fail through demand destruction, technology, policy, OPEC response or supply investment.
- “Public but obscure” data edge can decay as tools automate extraction.
- Sector underweight can persist structurally rather than mean-revert.

## YK read

Best insight: **buy company fundamentals on way up; buy macro fear on way down.** Do not confuse falling stock with widening margin of safety when operations worsen.

Young’s durable edge appears to be sandbox selection: small, hated, technically complex companies where few institutions compete. Alpha then comes from correct normalization, capital-allocation judgment and willingness to endure volatility.

For one-man hedge fund:

- separate commodity view from company edge;
- require returns at conservative oil deck;
- track debt/cash-flow inflection and per-share NAV;
- normalize well data per lateral, acreage and capital employed;
- size from expected shortfall, not conviction rhetoric;
- use starter positions until operating confirmation;
- treat obscure public filings as research surface;
- demand audited fund returns before accepting headline alpha.

## Bottom line

Young’s message: **chase edge, not hot-sector returns.** In neglected energy equities, buy discounted assets with improving capital allocation and survivable balance sheets; let debt paydown and cash returns compound; use macro only as small overlay. Compelling structural rationale, but concentrated cyclicality and unverified performance remain decisive caveats.
