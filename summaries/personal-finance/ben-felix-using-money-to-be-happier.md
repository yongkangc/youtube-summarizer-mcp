# Using Your Money To Be Happier

## TLDR

Personal finance should fund good life, not maximize terminal wealth. Money helps most by providing security, control and time, then enabling relationships, meaningful experiences, growth and health. Bigger income, house or retirement number often disappoints because people adapt, compare upward and mispredict future preferences.

## Good-life model `[1:51]`

Felix distinguishes:

- **Experienced/hedonic happiness:** how good life feels moment to moment.
- **Reflective/eudaimonic happiness:** how positively person evaluates life overall.

PERMA-V organizes common contributors:

- **P**ositive emotion;
- **E**ngagement/flow;
- **R**elationships;
- **M**eaning/service beyond self;
- **A**ccomplishment;
- **V**itality: sleep, nutrition, exercise.

Good life needs both pleasant days and worthwhile direction. Beer in hot tub may feel good without producing satisfying life; prestigious life can look good while feeling miserable daily.

## Income and happiness `[3:48]`

Research progression cited:

- 2010 study: life evaluation kept rising with income; experienced happiness plateaued near `$75K` then—roughly `$112K` inflation-adjusted.
- 2018 study: estimated North American satiation around `$105K` for life evaluation and `$65K` for experienced happiness—roughly `$137K` and `$85K` today.
- 2021 real-time sampling: both measures rose without observed plateau.
- 2023 adversarial collaboration: happier people continued benefiting at high incomes; already-unhappy people showed plateau.

Important scale correction: studies use **log income**. Each step means roughly doubling income. Relationship remains small: correlation around `0.09`; median happiness difference between `$15K` and `$250K` household income was about `5/100` points in cited sampling data.

Conclusion: enough money matters greatly; beyond security, extra income helps less than intuition predicts. Pursuit of money, fame and image can hurt through stress, less leisure, materialism and comparison.

## Why financial goals disappoint `[8:28]`

- **Hedonic adaptation:** happiness burst from lottery, house or purchase fades.
- **Focusing illusion:** imagine asset/goal, ignore how ownership changes daily time.
- **End-of-history illusion:** know past self changed, assume current self is permanent.
- **Social comparison:** richer peer group moves reference point and spending pressure.

People adapt poorly to some recurring negatives: noisy environment, stressful commute and lack of control. Bigger house farther from work may be bad exchange because owner adapts to space but not necessarily commute.

## Better spending decisions `[11:48]`

### Buy time

People prioritizing time over money tend to report greater happiness, connection and relationship quality. Outsourcing cleaning, reducing hours or paying for convenience can be rational when recovered time feeds PERMA-V.

### Housing

Homeownership may provide stability and control, but cited Canadian, Swiss, US and German evidence does not show large happiness premium after controls. Ownership adds maintenance and can reduce enjoyable time.

Ask not “rent or own?” abstractly. Ask which option produces better daily life after commute, maintenance, flexibility, control and total cost.

### Experiences over static possessions

Positive experiences generally outperform things because they:

- create engagement;
- are shared with others;
- generate anticipation and memory;
- resist adaptation because each is unique.

Negative experiences can do opposite. Material purchase can still be worthwhile when it changes time use meaningfully.

### Save, but do not postpone whole life

Financial independence creates control and security. Yet extreme saving competes with life today. Enjoyable work itself supplies engagement, meaning and accomplishment; retirement is not universally superior.

Prefer frequent small experiences linked to PERMA-V: coffee savored, hobby, dinner with friend, community support, course or physical activity.

## Regret `[17:06]`

Common regrets: relationships, family, education, career, finances, parenting and health. Action regret tends to fade; inaction regret often persists.

Implication: reversible opportunities—business, career move, relationship investment—deserve more weight than short-term fear suggests. But daily health and saving choices compound quietly until hard to reverse.

## Better goal setting `[19:02]`

Surface goal like “retire early” may conceal actual needs: autonomy, time with family, meaningful work or health. Felix's 2022 study found people generated deeper goals after seeing PERMA-V categories and master list of others' goals.

Process:

1. List initial financial goals.
2. Double list—force less obvious goals.
3. Review each PERMA-V category.
4. Ask how each goal changes daily time.
5. Revisit periodically because future self changes.
6. Fund underlying life objective, not symbolic asset.

## Audit / caveats

- Much cited evidence is observational and self-reported; association does not prove income, homeownership or time preference caused happiness.
- Income thresholds vary by year, geography, household size and methodology; do not use as universal salary caps.
- “Experiences beat things” depends on experience quality and personal fit.
- “Prioritize time” is constrained by income security, caregiving and career stage.
- Inaction regret is not license for YOLO risk; evaluate downside, reversibility and survival.

## Operator rule

> For every large expense or career choice, ask: **What will this make me do with my days, and which PERMA-V element does that improve?**

Money is scorekeeping only when detached from life. Real objective is enough security plus time, relationships, health, meaning and agency.
