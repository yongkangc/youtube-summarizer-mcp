# This Paper Could Change How You Invest

## TL;DR

Ben Felix explains why Fama and French's 1993 three-factor paper replaced the idea that market beta alone explains expected stock returns. Market, size and value exposures explained much more of diversified portfolio returns and showed that much apparent active-manager alpha was systematic factor exposure.

## Theory of edge

Factor investors may earn higher expected returns for bearing risks or tolerating unpopular characteristics that other investors avoid. Whether value, size, profitability and investment premiums are compensation for risk or persistent behavioral mispricing remains unresolved; historical average returns alone are not an edge.

## From CAPM to three factors

CAPM ties expected return only to market beta. It struggled with three observations:

- Small companies historically outperformed large companies beyond what beta predicted.
- High book-to-market, or value, stocks outperformed growth stocks beyond what beta predicted.
- The relationship between beta and average return was weaker than CAPM predicted.

Fama and French added:

- **Market:** broad equity-market exposure.
- **SMB, small minus big:** return difference between small and large companies.
- **HML, high minus low:** return difference between high and low book-to-market companies.

They tested 25 portfolios formed from five size groups crossed with five book-to-market groups over July 1963 to December 1991.

## Main result

CAPM explained roughly 60% of return variation in the test portfolios; the three-factor model raised explanatory power to around 90%, with near-zero regression intercepts for most portfolios. Small-growth stocks were the important exception: their returns were materially worse than the model explained.

Portfolios sorted on dividend yield and earnings yield were also largely explained by the same factors. This suggests dividend strategies are not a separate free return source; much of their performance is exposure to value and related systematic characteristics.

## What changed

- Portfolio performance should be evaluated relative to factor exposures, not only against a market index.
- An active manager who beats the market by holding small/value stocks may be delivering replicable factor exposure rather than security-selection alpha.
- Investors can target systematic expected-return sources through diversified, rules-based portfolios instead of paying traditional active-management fees.
- Later research added **profitability** (`RMW`, robust minus weak) and **investment** (`CMA`, conservative minus aggressive). Felix calls the resulting five-factor model the academic workhorse, explaining closer to 95% of diversified portfolio-return variation.

## Practical application

Felix points to Dimensional Fund Advisors and Avantis as firms implementing factor research through diversified, relatively low-cost funds. His firm, PWL Capital, uses Dimensional extensively; he states that neither Dimensional nor Avantis paid for the mention.

A sensible implementation is a broad global equity core with modest, diversified tilts toward value, profitability, conservative investment and selected smaller companies. The tilt only makes sense if the investor can tolerate long periods of underperformance without abandoning it.

## Caveats

- Explanatory power is not guaranteed future premium.
- The paper identifies common return patterns; it cannot resolve whether they represent risk compensation or market inefficiency because of the joint-hypothesis problem.
- Factor premiums can disappear for years or decades.
- Trading costs, taxes, turnover, fund design and crowding determine whether a theoretical premium reaches the investor.
- Size alone is not automatically attractive; low-quality small-growth stocks were the model's clearest failure.
- Adding many backtested factors invites data mining. Prefer economically defensible, independently replicated factors.

## YK read

Useful framework, not a command to replace stock selection with factor ETFs. It provides a harder benchmark: any claimed alpha should first be decomposed into market, value, size, profitability and investment exposure. If returns disappear after that adjustment, there was no distinct manager edge.

**One-line conclusion:** Most diversified portfolio return differences are systematic exposures, so pay active fees only for returns that survive factor adjustment.
