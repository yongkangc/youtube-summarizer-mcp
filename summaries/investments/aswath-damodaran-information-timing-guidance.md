# Information Timing and Release: The Gaming of Guidance

## Source

- Author: Aswath Damodaran
- Article: https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/07/information-timing-and-release-gaming.html
- Published: July 29, 2026
- Companion video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0pTXwulvxzA

## One-sentence thesis

More disclosure is not always more information: preserve concise quarterly financial reporting because it helps valuation and limits insider advantage, but remove bloated corporate guidance and reduce Federal Reserve forward guidance because both invite gaming and distract markets from fundamentals.

## The common problem

Damodaran links two apparently separate debates:

1. whether the SEC should replace mandatory quarterly corporate reporting with semiannual reporting;
2. whether the Federal Reserve should provide less guidance about future policy.

Both involve removing scheduled information that markets use to update prices. Supporters say less guidance reduces short-term gaming; opponents say less information worsens volatility and price discovery.

Damodaran rejects a single answer for both. The value depends on the content and source of information.

## Corporate reporting

### Historical context

- US annual reporting began under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934.
- Semiannual reporting was introduced in 1955.
- Quarterly reporting became mandatory in 1970.
- The NYSE required quarterly reporting for many firms as early as 1939.
- More than 60% of listed US firms reportedly disclosed quarterly information voluntarily in 1931.

This suggests firms may disclose frequently without a mandate because transparency can attract investors and lower perceived information risk.

Other markets have reversed quarterly mandates:

- UK: mandatory in 2007, optional again in 2014.
- EU: introduced in 2007, removed in 2013.
- Singapore: broad quarterly requirement from 2003; narrowed in 2020 to firms with regulatory/financial concerns.
- Japan: introduced in 2003; reversed in 2024.

Many firms still report quarterly voluntarily.

## The real problem is content, not frequency

Quarterly filings have expanded from roughly `2,000–5,000 words` in 1980 to several times that length.

Damodaran attributes the bloat to:

- expanding accounting requirements;
- additional disclosure after crises;
- global risk exposure;
- lawyer-written risk factors designed for legal protection;
- management guidance about future periods.

He calls this **"disclosure diarrhea"**: document volume rises while decision-useful information can fall.

Risk-factor boilerplate and management forecasts add less valuation value than the core financial statements and footnotes.

## How earnings guidance creates a game

The cycle is:

1. analysts estimate EPS, revenue and operating metrics;
2. services aggregate those estimates into consensus;
3. analysts revise forecasts as information arrives;
4. the company reports;
5. traders compare actual numbers against consensus;
6. prices react to the surprise, often followed by post-earnings drift.

Companies then learn to manage accounting choices and expectations to beat consensus.

This produces:

- disproportionately frequent positive surprises;
- analysts and companies mutually calibrating guidance;
- "whisper numbers" above official consensus;
- companies falling despite beating consensus because the hidden hurdle was higher;
- attention shifting from business value to a quarterly scorekeeping exercise.

By Q2 2026, Damodaran says the relationship between reported earnings surprises and immediate price reaction was weak, with many positive surprises followed by price declines.

## Quarterly versus semiannual reports

### Case for semiannual reporting

- reduces fixation on the next quarter;
- lowers managerial incentive to manipulate expectations;
- may encourage longer-term capital allocation;
- lowers reporting burden.

### Case for quarterly reporting

- gives investors current financial and operating information;
- helps valuations update, especially for young/high-growth firms;
- creates periodic price-correction catalysts;
- reduces the period during which insiders possess material private information;
- moving to semiannual reporting may merely concentrate the same gaming into two larger events.

### Damodaran's recommendation

Preserve quarterly reporting, but radically slim it down:

- keep financial statements;
- keep useful footnotes;
- retain operating metrics where business conditions change rapidly;
- remove boilerplate risk sections;
- reduce management guidance and forward-looking theater;
- replace one-size-fits-all disclosure with targeted information.

Quarterly updates matter most for young, high-growth companies because revenue, margins and unit economics can change rapidly. Mature firms may see little intrinsic-value movement from one quarter.

If the SEC removes the mandate, Damodaran expects many companies to keep reporting quarterly voluntarily.

## Short-term traders are not the enemy

Damodaran rejects "short-termism" as a useful regulatory target:

- most participants trade for near-term gain;
- regulation cannot remove that motivation;
- opportunistic trading can still aggregate into rational long-term prices;
- short-term traders supply liquidity and lower transaction costs for long-term investors.

The better target is bad disclosure design, not short investment horizons.

## Federal Reserve guidance

Damodaran argues investors substantially overestimate the Fed's control over rates and economic growth.

### What the Fed directly controls

- the Fed Funds rate;
- open-market operations;
- balance-sheet size;
- policy communication.

Its broad data access also makes policy decisions a signal about what officials see in inflation and growth.

### What markets control

The rates that drive most asset valuations and borrowing decisions are market-set:

- Treasury bills;
- longer-term Treasury yields;
- mortgage and corporate borrowing rates.

Damodaran's 1962–2026 data suggest:

- Fed Funds and short-term market rates are correlated;
- short-term Treasury rates often move before Fed policy;
- much of the rate move occurs before or during the policy quarter;
- there is little subsequent spillover into long rates;
- Fed policy changes often reflect market moves rather than cause them.

He also finds a weak historical relationship between Fed Funds changes and subsequent real GDP growth, outside exceptional interventions such as Volcker's 20% rates in 1981.

His description of the average Fed effect on the real economy: more **"meh" than "wow."**

## What determines long-term rates

Damodaran argues long rates mostly reflect:

- expected inflation;
- real economic growth;
- associated risk/term premia.

A Fed chair cannot sustainably force long-term rates to 2% while inflation expectations run at `2.5–3%`.

Treating the Fed as omnipotent is dangerous because:

- politicians demand impossible rate outcomes;
- investors ignore inflation and growth fundamentals;
- inappropriate rate cuts can increase inflation;
- markets outsource independent judgment to central-bank communication.

## Why Fed guidance should shrink

Explicit forward guidance expanded after the 2008 crisis and made the Fed central to every market narrative.

Damodaran recommends:

- fewer forecasts about future policy;
- fewer public opinions from individual FOMC members;
- greater humility about the Fed's limited powers;
- honesty that policy often follows markets;
- less investor attention to meeting language and "smoke signals."

Markets successfully set rates before the forward-guidance era. Removing guidance forces investors to price inflation, growth and risk directly, and the Fed can then learn from those market signals.

## Practical implications for investors

### Earnings

Ignore the beat/miss headline. Update valuation using:

- revenue trajectory;
- operating margins;
- reinvestment;
- unit economics;
- cash flows;
- balance sheet;
- durable changes to competitive position.

Quarterly price dislocations can be useful when the price reaction is larger than the change in intrinsic value.

### Rates

Focus less on FOMC phrasing and more on:

- inflation expectations;
- real growth;
- market-set short rates;
- ten-year Treasury yield;
- term/risk premium.

The Fed's action may be confirmation of a market move already underway rather than new causal information.

## Theory of edge

> Investors can exploit markets' fixation on scheduled narratives by distinguishing real valuation information from expectation-management noise.

Potential manifestations:

- Buy a strong business after a superficial EPS miss when revenue, margins and intrinsic-value drivers remain intact.
- Sell or avoid a stock after an engineered beat when underlying economics deteriorate.
- Do not trade rate-sensitive assets merely from Fed language when inflation, growth and market yields disagree.

This is a behavioral/information-processing edge, not a mechanical earnings-surprise strategy.

## Risks to the argument

- Less frequent reporting can reduce managerial reporting burden and potentially improve long-term planning.
- Forward guidance can stabilize expectations during crises or at the zero lower bound.
- Market prices can become disorderly when policymakers withhold reaction functions.
- Voluntary reporting may be least likely among companies where investors most need transparency.
- Slimmer disclosure can remove details that become useful only during tail events.

## Bottom line

Damodaran's position is not simply "less information." It is:

- **Corporate reporting:** keep it quarterly but concise, factual and backward-looking.
- **Corporate guidance:** reduce it because it fuels expectation gaming.
- **Fed communication:** provide less future-policy guidance and admit institutional limits.
- **Investor behavior:** price businesses from fundamentals and rates from inflation/growth, not quarterly theater or central-bank choreography.
