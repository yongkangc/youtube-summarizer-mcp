# Citadel Securities: Elastic Expectations

## TLDR

Citadel Securities sees tentative evidence that AI demand is elastic enough for falling intelligence costs to increase total ecosystem spending. Usage-weighted token prices fell **40% since end-June**, while July AI spend per employee rose **49% month on month among the top 1% of Ramp firms**, **25% among the top 10%**, and **9% at the median**. Meanwhile, GPU rental prices recovered from June lows and hyperscaler cloud revenue remained strong.[1]

The implication is bullish for aggregate compute demand but not automatically for frontier-model rents. Cheaper intelligence can expand usage while open-weight substitution compresses closed-model pricing power.[1]

## Theory of edge

**The market may be underestimating demand elasticity: falling cost per useful AI output could create enough additional consumption to sustain compute demand even as unit token prices collapse.**

## Evidence

### 1. Lower token prices, higher enterprise expenditure

- Usage-weighted token prices declined **40% from end-June**.[1]
- July AI spend per employee increased:[1]
  - **49% MoM** among the top 1% of firms;
  - **25% MoM** among the top 10%;
  - **9% MoM** at the median.
- Since October 2023, monthly spend per employee increased by **$6,542** at the top 1% versus **$9.63** at the median.[1]
- The ratio of 90th-percentile to median spending doubled from **27× to 54×**.[1]

Citadel's read: companies that already know how to deploy AI respond most aggressively when intelligence gets cheaper. Adoption is therefore concentrating rather than diffusing evenly.[1]

### 2. Token and compute pricing have diverged

In June, both token prices and GPU rentals fell, raising concern that end demand was weakening. More recently, GPU rental prices rebounded while token prices continued falling. Citadel treats this divergence as tentative evidence that greater usage is absorbing underlying compute capacity.[1]

Reported compute indicators:[1]

- H100 spot prices were about **13% above 36-month term rates** in mid-July.
- B200 spot premium was approximately **8%**.
- H100 pricing fell **41% from its May peak**, then recovered **20% from June lows**.
- Kalshi-implied B200 pricing was around **$6.17/hour**, above its May peak when the article was written.

The curves remain backwardated, consistent with expected capacity additions and hardware obsolescence, but not at levels Citadel considers alarming.[1]

### 3. Measure useful output, not raw tokens

Tokens differ by model capability and task. Citadel argues the economically relevant price is **cost per useful unit of work**, not token price alone.[1]

Capable open-weight models let users substitute away from expensive closed models without proportional loss of output. This creates two simultaneous effects:[1]

- more total intelligence consumption and compute demand;
- lower rents for closed-source frontier labs.

Thus ecosystem growth does not guarantee equal economics for every layer.

### 4. Earnings and cloud demand remain strong

- Q2 S&P 500 EPS growth was tracking around **33%**, which Citadel describes as the strongest pace outside post-recession recoveries.[1]
- Hyperscaler cloud revenue reached approximately **$106.3 billion in Q2 2026**, up **43% YoY** and **15% QoQ**.[1]

Citadel interprets this as evidence that recently installed compute is finding paying demand, although cloud revenue is broader than AI alone.

### 5. Broad macro markets do not look euphoric

Citadel's cross-asset growth factor, PC1, stood at **+0.84 standard deviations**, only the **65th percentile** of its five-year history.[1]

Its conclusion: despite strong AI rhetoric and earnings, broad asset markets are not pricing an extreme growth impulse. This does **not** establish that individual AI equities or compute assets are cheap.

## Investment read-through

### Potential beneficiaries

- Hyperscalers that convert cheaper inference into higher cloud consumption.
- GPU and compute infrastructure if usage growth continues to outpace efficiency gains.
- Model-serving, routing and orchestration layers that move workloads to the cheapest adequate model.
- Enterprises already able to turn AI consumption into measurable workflow output.

### Pressure points

- Closed frontier labs if open-weight models become good enough for most workloads.
- Compute owners underwriting scarcity pricing far into the future.
- Vendors whose revenue depends on token price rather than quantity growth.
- Late adopters: spending acceleration is concentrated among the most advanced firms, potentially widening productivity gaps.

## What the article does not prove

The Jevons conclusion is promising but preliminary:

- The key evidence covers recent weeks and one strong month, not a full cycle.
- Ramp's AI-spend measure combines subscriptions, coding agents, API tokens and GPU cloud; it is not a clean quantity-of-tokens series.[1]
- Comparing a token-price basket with enterprise spending does not yield a formal demand-elasticity estimate.
- Selecting the "top 1%" by current spending can amplify concentration and composition effects.
- Token-price indices require quality adjustment as model mix changes.
- Firm GPU rentals may reflect delayed supply, contracts or temporary scarcity rather than Jevons demand.
- Hyperscaler cloud revenue contains non-AI workloads.
- Broad cross-asset restraint does not rule out exuberance within AI-linked securities.

## KPIs and kill tests

Track:

1. AI spending growth at the median, not only the top 1%.
2. Token volumes and completed-work units versus token prices.
3. GPU rental utilization and spot/term curves by chip generation.
4. Hyperscaler AI revenue separately from aggregate cloud revenue.
5. Open-weight share of production workloads.
6. Closed-model API gross margins and realized price per useful task.
7. Enterprise ROI: labor saved or revenue created per AI dollar.
8. Compute additions versus incremental paying demand.

The thesis fails if:

- token prices keep falling while enterprise AI spend and task volumes flatten;
- GPU rental prices/utilization roll over despite cheaper inference;
- expenditure remains confined to a tiny upper tail without median adoption;
- architectural efficiency reduces compute per task faster than task demand expands;
- hyperscaler cloud growth decelerates once capacity additions arrive.

## Bottom line

Citadel has identified the right variable: **elasticity, not token price alone**. July data tentatively support Jevons-style demand, but one month's spending and noisy young markets are not enough to prove that today's compute capex will earn acceptable returns.
