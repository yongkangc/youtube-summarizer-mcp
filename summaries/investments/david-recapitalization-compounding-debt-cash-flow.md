# David Rekuc: Compounding, Productive Debt and Cash-Flow Businesses

## TLDR

The video combines several personal-finance and business-investing lessons around one principle: maximize long-run compounding of real cash flow. Favor businesses with moats, pricing power, low capital intensity and disciplined capital allocation. Use debt only when it funds productive assets and remains survivable under adverse rates and cash flows.

The useful framework is sound. Several numerical shortcuts are promotional or incomplete: EBITDA is not cash flow; buying at 3× EBITDA does not automatically create a 33% return; borrowing at 5% to chase an expected 10% equity return is not a guaranteed 5% spread; and extreme business returns are subject to leverage, workload and survivorship bias.

## 1. Business metrics

### EBITDA

EBITDA excludes interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It can make an equipment-heavy business appear healthy while replacement capex consumes cash.

Use EBITDA as an operating-comparison metric, not cash available to owners. Reconcile it to:

- maintenance and growth capex;
- working-capital investment;
- cash taxes;
- interest;
- stock-based compensation and dilution;
- owner compensation.

### Margin stack

He distinguishes:

- **Gross margin:** revenue minus cost of goods sold, including outbound shipping in his definition.
- **Contribution margin:** gross profit minus variable selling, fulfillment, labor and marketing costs.
- **Net margin:** profit after overhead, interest, taxes and brand marketing.

He prefers businesses with at least **50% gross margin**, arguing this creates room for marketing and scaling. That threshold is business-model specific. Distribution, grocery and industrial businesses can compound successfully with low gross margins and high inventory turns.

## 2. Beta, alpha and edge

- **Beta:** growth inherited from market or industry exposure.
- **Alpha:** return or business growth beyond relevant benchmark.

His advice: enter growing industries so average execution still receives a tailwind, then build company-specific alpha.

**Theory of edge:** above-market returns require an unfair advantage, such as local knowledge, operating skill, sourcing, customer relationships or proprietary systems. Without identifiable edge, accept low-cost public-market beta.

A local real-estate or small-business edge may support higher returns, but these are concentrated operating jobs—not passive equivalents to an index fund.

## 3. Expected value and asymmetric bets

He recommends positive-expected-value opportunities with capped downside and large upside, sized to preserve ability to continue playing.

Illustrations:

- 95% chance to gain $500 and 5% chance to lose $1 million: strongly negative expected value.
- 95% chance to lose $5 and 5% chance to gain $1 million: strongly positive expected value.

He references Kelly sizing. Correct application requires calibrated probabilities, payoff distributions and correlation. Full Kelly is often too aggressive when estimates are uncertain; fractional Kelly is safer.

Patience matters because investors can decline opportunities without penalty. Opportunity cost means every deployment prevents another use of the capital.

## 4. Return ladder

### 3%: cash-like safety

A 3% nominal return may lose purchasing power after inflation and taxes. Cash still has option value for emergencies and future opportunities, so low return does not make all cash irrational.

### 7%: diversified equity compounding

He uses approximately 7% as the S&P 500's long-run real return. At 7%, $100,000 compounds to about **$761,000** over 30 years.

This is appropriate default territory for investors without differentiated edge.

### 14%: local or operating edge

At 14%, $100,000 compounds to about **$5.10 million** over 30 years. He suggests local real estate or small businesses where personal knowledge creates edge.

These returns require work, concentration and illiquidity; comparison with passive equities should adjust for labor and risk.

### 30%: owner-operated businesses

He argues small businesses bought around 3× EBITDA can produce roughly 30% returns and reinvest internally.

This is too mechanical. A 3× EBITDA purchase is not automatically a 33% return because EBITDA excludes capex, working capital, taxes and financing. Return should use normalized free cash flow divided by total invested capital.

### 60%: early-stage scale

He says Filterbuy compounded above 60% while revenue was roughly $1–10 million. Such rates become harder at scale. He now targets approximately 30%.

Correct lesson: reinvestment runway and incremental return on capital matter more than historical percentage growth.

### 120%: venture power law

One extreme winner may pay for many failures. His stylized VC portfolio has 18 failures, one capital return and one 100× winner.

Most investors lack access, selection skill and enough bets. He personally prefers compounding his controlled businesses to venture-style outcomes.

## 5. Compounding and numerical caveats

The penny-doubling example is mathematically correct:

- day-30 payment: approximately **$5.37 million**;
- total over 30 days: approximately **$10.74 million**.

One claim is not: $1 million growing at 20% for 20 years becomes approximately **$38.3 million**, not $64 million.

Likewise, $300,000 compounded at 30% for 12 years becomes about **$7.0 million**. Reaching hundreds of millions requires additional leverage, external capital, higher returns or operating growth beyond that simple model.

## 6. Productive versus destructive debt

- **Destructive debt:** finances consumption or depreciating lifestyle assets without cash flow.
- **Productive debt:** finances assets expected to appreciate or produce cash.

He uses 7% as a rough borrowing-cost cutoff and argues that paying off 5% debt may be inferior to retaining equity exposure expected to return 10%.

This comparison is incomplete:

- loan cost may be certain while equity return is volatile;
- taxes reduce investment return;
- market drawdowns create sequence risk;
- margin or securities-backed loans can be callable;
- refinancing and floating-rate exposure can destroy an otherwise good asset.

Correct rule: compare debt cost with a conservative, after-tax, risk-adjusted asset return and maintain liquidity under stress—not average historical equity returns.

## 7. Buy, borrow, die

Wealthy owners may avoid realizing capital gains by borrowing against appreciating assets. Under current US rules, heirs may receive a stepped-up tax basis at death.

This is jurisdiction-specific and carries:

- collateral-call risk;
- interest-rate risk;
- estate-law change risk;
- concentration risk;
- cash-flow requirements for interest.

It is not a general instruction to maximize leverage.

## 8. Interest rates and bonds

Government bond yields set the opportunity cost of capital. Higher risk-free rates:

- make risky assets less attractive;
- raise business financing costs;
- pressure discretionary spending;
- reduce valuations;
- increase the return hurdle for new projects.

Businesses should earn materially more than the risk-free rate after adjusting for risk and illiquidity. Nominal profit alone is insufficient.

## 9. Four durable business attributes

His final filter:

1. **Moat:** brand, process, geography, reputation or relationships that resist competition.
2. **Pricing power:** ability to raise prices without losing customers.
3. **Low capital intensity:** growth does not consume an equal amount of incremental capital.
4. **Disciplined capital allocation:** every dollar has a purpose, timeline and expected return.

These support high incremental returns and long reinvestment runways.

## YK read

Best operating rule:

> Own a cash-generating machine with structural edge, reinvest at high incremental returns, and use only debt the cash flows can survive.

Underwriting checklist:

- normalized free cash flow, not EBITDA;
- incremental ROIC, not historical revenue growth;
- reinvestment runway;
- maintenance capex and working-capital intensity;
- pricing power and customer retention;
- fixed versus floating debt;
- debt-service coverage under recession and rate stress;
- owner labor included as economic cost;
- expected return versus current risk-free hurdle;
- downside survival before upside compounding.

## Bottom line

Strong principles, loose arithmetic. Wealth compounds through real cash flow, durable edge and disciplined reinvestment—not headline EBITDA or return targets. Debt accelerates a sound machine but also accelerates failure when asset cash flow, refinancing or collateral assumptions break.
