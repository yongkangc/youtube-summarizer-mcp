## TLDR

Dr. Jim ranks permanent long puts as the least attractive portfolio hedge because their option premium continually decays and successful use requires timing the selloff. His preferred hedge is not another option structure but entering every position small enough that no individual loss or volatility shock threatens the portfolio.

## Hedge ranking

### 1. Position sizing: best

- Defined-risk trade allocation: approximately `1–3%` at entry.
- Undefined-risk allocation: approximately `3–7%` at entry.
- These are tastylive sizing guidelines; buying-power allocation for undefined risk is not maximum loss.
- Small positions preserve liquidity, reduce forced adjustment, and allow probabilities to play out across many occurrences.
- The cost is opportunity cost: smaller positions also produce smaller gains.

### 2. Static short delta

- Useful mainly for a short-premium portfolio.
- Equity selloffs typically combine negative delta with volatility expansion; short delta profits from the price decline and offsets some short-vega losses.
- Dr. Jim's companion framework references approximately `1 unit of short delta per 2 units of theta` as a portfolio guide.
- Cost: equities drift upward over time, so continuously carrying short delta creates drag.

### 3. Long VIX calls

- Can pay convexly during volatility spikes.
- Hard to execute because the timing and strike/expiry must be right.
- Repeatedly holding or rolling them incurs decay and can consume substantial premium before the shock arrives.

### 4. Long SPY puts: least favorite

- They provide direct crash protection if the decline arrives soon.
- But downside skew makes puts structurally expensive.
- If the market rises or remains stable, theta and implied-volatility normalization erode them.
- Rolling protection indefinitely repeatedly crystallizes premium expense.

## Theory of edge and cost

There is no free hedge. Put and VIX-call buyers pay option sellers to transfer crash convexity and timing risk. Static short delta replaces option decay with persistent bearish carry. Position sizing does not profit during a crash, but lowers the quantity of risk that needs hedging in the first place.

## YK read

The durable lesson is correct: size is the first risk control; overlays are secondary. But `1–3%` per defined-risk position can still be dangerous when twenty trades share the same short-volatility or equity-beta factor. Size the whole portfolio by stressed correlated loss, not each ticker independently.
