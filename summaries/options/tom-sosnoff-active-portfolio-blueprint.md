# Tom Sosnoff's Active-Portfolio Blueprint

## TL;DR

Sosnoff proposes a capital-efficient active book mixing stocks, futures and options, with many small, liquid, low-correlated positions. He targets positive theta and a 65–72% modeled portfolio probability of profit, then manages exposure using buying-power usage and SPY beta-weighted delta.

## Theory of edge

The implied edge is short-volatility carry and liquidity provision: systematically sell option premium whose implied volatility exceeds subsequent realized volatility, while diversification, small sizing and early management contain path and tail risk. Leverage, theta and high probability of profit are not themselves edge.

## Blueprint

- Approximate buying-power mix: **20% stocks, 20% futures/futures options, 50% options, 10% cash**. Sosnoff calls this illustrative rather than fixed.
- Diversify by underlying, asset class, strategy, implied volatility and duration—not merely by owning several stocks.
- Normalize the portfolio into **SPY beta-weighted deltas** so unlike positions can be compared and hedged.
- Use liquid products; liquidity improves execution and the reliability of model Greeks.
- Prefer numerous small positions, manage early and repeat the process mechanically.

## Capital and trade rules presented

- Low-volatility regime (`VIX 10–15`): total buying-power use around **25% conservative, 35% moderate, 45–50% aggressive**.
- `VIX >40`: roughly **50%, 65%, 75–80%**, respectively, because richer premium permits greater deployment.
- Defined-risk trade allocation: generally **0.5–3%**, with **5% maximum** cited.
- Undefined-risk single-underlying allocation: approximately **1–7%**, with as much as **10–15%** cited for small accounts.
- Portfolio modeled POP target: **65–72%**.
- Suggested daily theta: **0.1–0.3% of net liquidation value**.
- Expected move supplies strike/exit context; volatility estimates magnitude, not direction.
- Skew identifies where the market prices faster risk, not where price will move.

## Stock versus options example

For a hypothetical `$100` stock:

- 100 shares require `$10,000` without margin and have roughly 50/50 directional exposure.
- A 30-day, 50-delta long call costs about `$363`, but needs a larger rally to clear breakeven.
- A 30-day, 50-delta short put uses about `$2,300` buying power and is presented with roughly **65% POP**.

The short put improves breakeven and capital efficiency by selling upside beyond the strike and retaining substantial downside. Its higher POP does not prove higher expected or risk-adjusted return.

## Critical audit

- **Buying-power reduction is margin, not maximum risk.** It can expand sharply during stress, especially for naked and futures options.
- **Law of large numbers only reveals expectancy; it cannot make a negative-expectancy strategy profitable.** Sosnoff's suggested 500–1,500+ annual trades also create commissions and slippage.
- **High POP hides negative skew.** Small frequent wins can be erased by gaps, volatility expansion and forced deleveraging.
- Cross-asset correlations commonly converge toward one during crashes; “non-correlated” short-vol positions may become the same trade.
- The suggested **0.1–0.3% daily theta** and up to **15% undefined-risk allocation** are aggressive. They require portfolio-level delta, gamma, vega, liquidity and stress-loss limits—not only BPR.
- The video title says 2025, but the webinar itself repeatedly frames its material as 2024.

## YK rule

Use the durable parts: small units, liquid products, beta-weighted exposure, multiple asset classes and volatility-aware capital deployment. Reject BPR and POP as sufficient risk controls. Size from stressed portfolio loss and expected shortfall first; treat theta as compensation for warehousing convexity and crash risk.

**One-line conclusion:** capital efficiency helps diversification, but without positive expectancy and hard tail-risk gates, it merely enables more leverage.
